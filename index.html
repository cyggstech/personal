The story

A smart kid with grit, and nothing else.

I never finished high school. I never finished college either. What I had was a habit of building the thing in front of me until it worked, and then selling it.

My first real company, Shadow Surveillance, founded in 2012, put cameras and monitoring on job sites that insurers and contractors had written off as unprotectable. I sold it. I built the next one, Jobsite Sentry, around what I'd learned: AI-enhanced surveillance towers that watch construction and industrial sites around the clock, now deployed across the United States. Advanced Industrial Monitoring came in 2020. Across twelve years the three have grossed more than $40 million. None of it was inherited, raised, or lucky.

Along the way I designed and directed the technology myself: a fleet of more than 300 solar- and Starlink-powered surveillance units with AI false-alarm filtering and our own NVIDIA Jetson recorder; a 24/7 monitoring center; the Jobsite Sentry and AIMonit apps on the App Store and Google Play, with live PTZ video, LiDAR scanning and measurement, YOLO object counting, an MSHA safety AI assistant and license-plate recognition; Command Center, our in-house ERP and CRM with AI quote generation, an AI phone receptionist and 3D site reconstruction, which replaced a ten-year-old platform in 2025; drone flyovers of working quarries; a Telegram blockchain game with its own token, pitched at Puerto Rico Blockchain Week; and a persona-chat AI engine on iOS, web and Telegram. Customers: D.R. Horton, Martin Marietta, Vulcan Materials, CalPortland, Holcim, Brookfield Properties, Meritage Homes. In 2024 I opened CygnusTech in Dubai Silicon Oasis to build all of it, and in 2025 and 2026 I went after the hardest problems I could find and filed three U.S. patent applications: a room-temperature probabilistic processor that couples spintronic devices to a photonic mesh, a computing architecture that programs the curvature of resonant structures instead of flipping logic gates, and a test for AI that claims to improve itself.

The test

Why I built a lie detector for self-improving AI.

Every AI company now says its model can improve itself. I wanted to know if mine could, so I spent a year trying to make it happen. Then I asked the harder question: if it looked like it worked, how would I know the improvement was real and not leaked answers, leftover memory, extra compute, or luck?

The answer is the Cisneros Transplant Test. Take the training recipe the AI wrote for itself. Hand it to a brand-new student that inherits nothing else. Grade it with an exam the AI has never seen. Run it head-to-head against the original recipe under identical conditions, with the rule for calling a winner fixed before anyone sees a score. If the improvement is real, it survives the handoff. If it isn't, it dies on the table.

I ran it on my own system. It said no: a simple fixed training rule beat the AI's rewrite on five of six seeds, twice. I published that, filed a U.S. patent application on the instrument, and released the method under a noncommercial license. Now I'm asking universities to run their own systems through it and publish whatever it says. A field full of self-improvement claims needs a lie detector more than it needs one more claim.
